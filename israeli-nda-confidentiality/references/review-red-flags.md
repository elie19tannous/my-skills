# Israeli NDA — Reviewer's Red-Flag Checklist

Use this when a user is asked to sign an NDA someone else drafted, before they sign.

## Structural red flags

- [ ] **One-way where it should be mutual.** If the recipient will also expose their own methods, know-how, or business information during the relationship, a one-way NDA protects only the drafter. Push for a mutual (hadadi) NDA, or at minimum a residual-knowledge/general-skills carve-out so ordinary professional expertise isn't captured as "confidential."
- [ ] **Unbounded definition of Confidential Information.** Watch for language covering "everything, forever," with no limiting principle. Ask for the standard exclusions (see below) and a defined, reasonable survival period.
- [ ] **Missing standard exclusions.** The definition should exclude: information already public, information the recipient already lawfully knew, information independently developed, information lawfully received from a third party, and information disclosure of which is compelled by law (with notice). Their absence signals an over-broad, drafter-favoring document.
- [ ] **Hidden or over-broad non-compete.** NDAs are frequently bundled with non-compete/non-solicit language. In Israel, a bare non-compete that only blocks ordinary competition (no genuine trade secret, no special training, no separate consideration) is very likely unenforceable under the Checkpoint/AES Supreme Court line. Flag it and push to strike or narrow it regardless — do not rely on "it probably won't hold up" as a reason to leave it in.
- [ ] **Penalty-style agreed damages.** A liquidated-damages figure disconnected from any realistic estimate of harm (e.g. a flat "500,000 NIS per breach" regardless of actual impact) is the kind of clause a court can reduce under the Contracts (Remedies for Breach) Law, 1970 — but it's still worth negotiating down, since litigating a reduction costs time and money.
- [ ] **Foreign governing law/forum.** For an agreement between Israeli parties, a foreign court or governing law clause (often copied from a US template) creates real cost and inconvenience if a dispute arises. Push for Israeli law and a convenient Israeli court or arbitration seat.

## Term and survival red flags

- [ ] **No distinction between disclosure period and confidentiality survival.** These are different clocks; conflating them (e.g. a 6-month engagement with only a 6-month confidentiality duty) underprotects information that should stay secret much longer.
- [ ] **A hard cap on survival for information that is a genuine trade secret.** If the recipient will be exposed to a real trade secret (sod mishari), an arbitrary short survival period (e.g. 1–2 years) effectively releases the secret to the world on a fixed date. The obligation should instead run for as long as the information remains secret and valuable.

## Personal data overlap

- [ ] **No data-protection clause where personal data is involved.** If the confidential information includes customer databases, employee records, or other PII, the Protection of Privacy Law, 1981 and Israel's data-security regulations apply on top of the NDA. A pure trade-secret NDA silent on this can miss real compliance obligations for both sides.

## Process

1. Read the definition of Confidential Information first — it drives everything else.
2. Check for the standard exclusions; flag any that are missing.
3. Check whether the NDA is one-way or mutual, and whether that matches the actual relationship.
4. Look for a bundled non-compete/non-solicit and assess Israeli enforceability separately from the confidentiality clause itself.
5. Check the term/survival structure — one clock or two, capped or indefinite for real secrets.
6. Check governing law/forum.
7. Recommend a licensed Israeli lawyer review before signing, especially if any red flag above is present.
